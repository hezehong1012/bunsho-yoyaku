A proofreading checklist for non-native English writers

Spell check catches misspellings. It does not catch the errors that actually cost credibility in a US or UK workplace, which are mostly grammatical and mostly boring: a missing article, a verb that does not agree, a preposition that is wrong in a way nobody can explain.

Pass 1 — articles and agreement

Read only for “a / an / the” and for singular-plural mismatches. “We need approval from the finance team” versus “we need the approval”. Both exist in English; the difference is whether approval is a thing or a process. When unsure, ask whether your reader already knows which one you mean.

Pass 2 — tense and aspect

Timeline errors cluster in the same paragraph. Mark every verb with the time it refers to, then check they agree. “We have shipped it last week” is the classic result of translating from a language with a simpler past.

Pass 3 — prepositions and phrasal verbs

Depend on, consist of, comply with, responsible for, discuss (no “about”), explain (needs “to”). These are memorisation, not logic. Keep a personal list of the ten you get wrong and check only those.

Pass 4 — punctuation that changes meaning

A comma splice (“we launched it, it worked”) is read as sloppy rather than wrong. A missing comma before “which” changes what the clause modifies. And a hyphen in “a ten year old policy” versus “a ten-year-old policy” changes the noun.

Pass 5 — consistency, not correctness

US or UK spelling, but not both. “Organisation” and “analyze” in the same document looks careless even though each is defensible. Dates the same way throughout; numbers formatted consistently.

Pass 6 — read it aloud

The last pass is ears. Anything you stumble over when speaking is a sentence that needs splitting.

Where a tool helps

Passes 1, 2 and 4 are mechanical, and a proofreader is reliable on them. Passes 3 and 5 benefit from it if you keep a short list of your own repeat errors. Pass 6 you still have to do yourself.
