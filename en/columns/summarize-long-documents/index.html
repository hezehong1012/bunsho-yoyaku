How to summarize a long document without losing the numbers

Most bad summaries fail for one reason: the summarizer did not know what the reader was going to do with it. A summary for a decision meeting needs the recommendation, the cost and the conditions. A summary for a colleague who might read the original later needs the shape of the argument. Same document, two different summaries.

1. Decide the job before you paste

Write one line first: “I need to decide whether to approve this”, or “I need to brief my manager in five sentences”. That line is what tells you which length to pick and whether bullets or prose is appropriate. In the summarizer, the “use it as” setting is that line — General, Report, Meeting notes, Email recap.

2. Keep hard facts pinned

Numbers, dates, names, obligations. These are the things a summary must not blur, and the things it most often rounds off. If three figures matter, put them in the “must include” field. It is the cheapest guarantee available.

3. Read the summary against three sentences of the source

Not the whole source — the opening and the recommendation. If the summary's conclusion does not match the document's conclusion, the error is usually in chunk merging: a long report gets sectioned, and a claim from section two can migrate to the top. Checking the first and last paragraph catches most of it.

4. Know when to stop summarizing

If the document is a contract, a regulatory filing or anything with a number you will be held to, do not rely on a generated summary for the number. Use it to find the page, then read the page.
