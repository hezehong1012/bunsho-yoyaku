Wedding toast examples: six short speeches that land

Every example below is a whole short toast, not a fragment — under 300 words, with a note on why it works and how long it runs. Swap the names and the one specific detail, and each one holds up.

2–3 minutes eachNames blankedNo borrowed punchlines

Before you borrow anything: the four beats

A toast that works is always the same shape: hook (six seconds that make people look up) → who they are to you (one line, so the room knows why you are talking) → one specific proof (a memory with a detail nobody else could tell) → the toast (short, forward-looking, everybody can join). The examples below are that shape, varied.

1. The gentle roast (best man, 2 minutes)

“I have known [Groom] for nineteen years, and in that time he has been late to everything except this. [Bride], the first time I met you he told me you were ‘the most organised person he had ever met’ — which, from a man who once missed a flight because he was looking for his passport in his own kitchen, sounded like panic. What he meant was: he had found someone who makes his life work. I have watched him get calmer, not smaller. That is the whole thing. To [Groom] and [Bride].”

Why it works: the joke is about the groom's own flaw that everyone already knows, so no one has to be embarrassed, and it turns into a compliment to the bride in the same sentence. The last two lines drop the joke entirely — that is what people remember.

About 130 words · 1 minute at a normal pace

2. The three-liner (the shortest version that still lands)

“I was told to keep this short, which is a shame, because I have a list. [Groom], you are the only person I know who reads the terms and conditions — and [Bride], you are the only person who could get him to stop. I give it forty years, minimum. To the two of you.”

Why it works: two jokes and a promise, in under a minute. Short toasts fail when they are only a joke; the closing line is what makes it a toast and not a heckle.

About 55 words · 30 seconds

3. The maid of honour (2.5 minutes)

“[Bride] and I met at twenty-three, in a flat with one working radiator and a fridge that held nothing but condiments. She was the one who kept the flat habitable, and she has been doing that for everyone she loves ever since. [Groom], you got the best of it. A year ago she rang me and said, ‘I think this is it’ — and she said it in the same voice she uses for a good spreadsheet, which, if you know her, is the highest compliment available. I have never seen her that certain about anything. Look after her spreadsheet heart.”

Why it works: the specific object (the radiator, the condiments, the spreadsheet voice) does the affection, so nothing has to be said twice. The inside joke is explained in half a clause, so guests outside the friendship still get it.

About 150 words · 1.5 minutes

4. The father of the bride (3 minutes, no jokes needed)

“Twenty-nine years ago I carried [Bride] into this house at two in the morning because she would not sleep unless the hallway light was on. Last spring she rang to tell me about [Groom], and she was standing in a hallway with the light on. Some things do not change, and I am glad of it. [Groom], you are not gaining a wife who needs looking after — she has never needed that. You are gaining someone who will look after you whether or not you asked. Be worth it.”

Why it works: one image repeated at the start and the end, no joke at all, and a line addressed directly to the groom that is warm but not sentimental. This is the version to use when the room is already crying and a joke would derail it.

About 130 words · 1 minute

5. The second marriage, older couple (2 minutes)

“[Groom] and [Bride], you two have done the hard part already — the divorces, the teenagers, the years where nobody was clapping for you. So I am not going to talk about a fresh start, because you did not get one; you got a better one. What I see is two people who know exactly what they are choosing, and are choosing it on a Tuesday afternoon, with the dog in the car. That is more romantic than anything I said at the first weddings. To the two of you, and to the dog.”

Why it works: it names the awkward thing (second marriages, past divorces) warmly and refuses to pretend, which is what guests in that situation are quietly hoping someone will do. The dog line ends it on a laugh so nobody has to sit in the heavy part.

About 135 words · 1 minute

6. The colleague toast (short, for a work wedding)

“I have sat in approximately four hundred meetings with [Bride], and the thing I know about her is that she is the person who says the true thing when everyone else is being polite. [Groom], the first time she mentioned you she did not say you were handsome, she said you were ‘reliable’. In this building, that is a love poem. To [Bride] and [Groom].”

Why it works: it stays inside the world the guests share (meetings, politeness) and translates a plain word into affection. Keep work toasts under 90 seconds — half the room does not know the couple and is waiting for the food.

About 90 words · 40 seconds

Lines that reliably die

“For those who don't know me…” (they don't care yet — start with the hook). “I'm going to keep this short.” (then be short). Any joke about an ex, a stripper, or a first marriage. Any line that needs the groom to explain it afterwards. And quoting a speech you found online word for word — the room can hear that it is not yours.

Timing

At a comfortable speaking pace you cover about 130–150 words a minute, so: 30 seconds ≈ 60 words, 2 minutes ≈ 280 words, 3 minutes ≈ 400. Print at 16pt, one sentence per line, and mark the two places you plan to pause — reading from a phone at arm's length is what makes a good toast sound rushed.

FAQ

How long should a wedding toast be?

Two to three minutes, roughly 260–400 words. Past five minutes you lose the room, particularly outdoors.

Can I use a joke I found online?

One borrowed line is fine if you rewrite the setup around the couple. Three in a row and the wording stops sounding like you.

What if I am not funny?

Use one specific memory instead of a joke. A concrete detail gets a warmer reaction than a joke you do not believe in.
